The meeting that
went great —
and then nothing.

The follow-up was perfect. The product was right. The energy in the room was real. So why did it go completely silent after? Everyone talks about closing. Nobody talks about what you're doing in the meeting that makes closing impossible. This is that.

I've had this happen more times than I'd like to admit.

A first meeting goes well. Really well. Good questions from their side, genuine back-and-forth, one of those rare hours where you leave thinking — that's what this is supposed to feel like. You send a tight follow-up that evening. And then you wait.

Nothing. Not a reply, not a "we'll be in touch," not even a polite no. Just silence where a deal used to be.

I told myself it was timing. Budget. An internal reshuffle. All of which were sometimes true. But the pattern kept repeating, and eventually I had to stop blaming circumstance and start asking what I was actually doing wrong.

◆

The pattern I almost missed

Because it happened again. And again. Great energy in the room, polite silence after. I started calling it the "great meeting curse" — like I had some talent for creating a wonderful hour that led absolutely nowhere. I kept pressure-testing it against new situations before I trusted what I was seeing. Eventually the signal was too consistent to ignore.

And this was my take

"Simi, a good meeting isn't a milestone. It's just a conversation that didn't hurt you yet. The real question is whether you gave them a reason to take the next step — or just a nice memory of you."

Then came the realization of What I thought I was doing versus what was actually happening

Here's the honest version of what was going on in those meetings — my internal experience versus what the customer was probably experiencing.

What I thought I was doing
Building rapport and trust by listening deeply
Showing product value through smart, relevant examples
Leaving the door open by not being pushy
Ending on a high note so they'd remember me positively
What the buyer probably experienced
A pleasant conversation with no clear outcome defined
Interesting, but no specific problem was diagnosed or named
No reason to do anything next — no urgency, no stakes
A meeting they'd have to explain to their boss without a concrete ask

See the gap? I was optimising for the vibe. They needed a reason. Not a pushy "so shall we set up a demo?" reason — but a genuine, specific, this is what happens next and why it matters reason.

A customer who leaves a great meeting with no clear next step doesn't think "I should follow up on that." They think "that was nice" — and then go back to the forty other things on their plate.

— What ghosting usually means, when you're honest about it

The real job of a discovery call

I used to think discovery was about understanding the client — their problems, their setup, their goals. And yes, it's that. But that's only half of it.

The other half — the half I was missing — is creating shared clarity about what happens next and why it matters to them right now.

The thing I now do in every discovery call

Before I wrap up, I ask one question that I used to be too polite to ask: "If this problem doesn't get solved in the next six months, what does that cost you?" Not in rupees — in whatever currency matters to them. Headcount. Hours. A failed product launch. A conversation they don't want to have with their board. When they answer that question out loud, the next step writes itself.

Three things I changed after those lost deals

None of these are radical. None of them require being pushy or salesy. They just require being intentional in a place where I used to coast on good energy.

  • 1
    Name the problem back to them, explicitly. Before we talk solutions, I now spend a minute summarising what I heard as their actual problem — in my words. "So what I'm hearing is… is that right?" It sounds simple. It's remarkably rare. And when they say "yes, exactly" — something shifts. You've just become the person who understood them, not just heard them.
  • 2
    Agree on a specific next step before you end the call. Not "I'll send you something." Not "let's find time again soon." Something concrete — "I'll send you a two-page summary of how we solved this for a team in a similar position, and you tell me if it's relevant." That email now has a reason to exist. They're expecting it. They'll open it.
  • 3
    Ask who else cares about this problem. Not to collect contacts. To understand if the person you're speaking to can actually move things forward, or if there's someone else who needs to be in the room. "Is this a problem that sits with your team, or is there a budget conversation that needs to happen upstream?" That question has saved me months of chasing the wrong person.
◆

About that contact who never moved things forward

I ran into them at an industry event some months later. We spoke briefly. They were warm, remembered everything, said something kind about the product.

And somewhere in the conversation it became clear — not from anything they said directly, but from what they didn't say — that the decision had never really been theirs to make. There were internal relationships, existing loyalties, considerations that were in play long before we walked in. The meeting had been genuine. The constraint had been structural.

Which told me something I hadn't been asking. Not just "is now a good time?" — but "is this person actually positioned to move this forward, and are they motivated to?" Those are different questions. The first is about timing. The second is about power and intent. I'd been asking the first. I should have been asking both.

What I'd do differently now

Before I wrap any discovery call, I now ask something I used to consider too forward: "If this went ahead — who else would need to be part of that decision?" That question either surfaces the real structure of how a decision gets made, or it tells you a lot about why someone is reluctant to answer it. Either way, you know where you actually stand. No more chasing ghosts.

That's the thing nobody tells you about enterprise sales: a no you can hear is infinitely more useful than a yes that disappears.

I'd rather know in the meeting that the dynamics aren't right than spend weeks crafting follow-ups into a void.

What I'd do differently now

Near the end of that original meeting, I'd ask: "Given everything on your plate right now — is this the kind of problem that needs solving this quarter, or is there something that needs to shift internally before it becomes a priority?" That one question either surfaces the real obstacle, or it locks in the urgency. Either way, you know where you actually stand. No more guessing. No more chasing ghosts.

That's the thing nobody tells you about sales early on: a no you can hear is infinitely more useful than a yes that disappears.

I'd rather know in the meeting that the timing is wrong than spend three weeks crafting emails into a void. Give me the honest conversation over the polite non-reply, every single time.

◆

Great meetings are a wonderful thing. I still love them. There is nothing like an hour where two people are genuinely thinking together about a real problem.

But I've learned to hold that energy lightly now — to enjoy it without mistaking it for progress. Progress is a named problem, a specific next step, and someone who knows what they're supposed to do tomorrow morning.

Everything else is just a nice conversation.

Next time you come out of a great meeting — before you write the follow-up, ask yourself: did we agree on something specific, or did I just leave them with a good feeling? The answer will tell you a lot.
